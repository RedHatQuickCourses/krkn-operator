Executing pod-disruption experiments

Executing Pod-Disruption Experiments

Pod-disruption experiments are the foundational building blocks of Chaos Engineering in Kubernetes. By intentionally terminating pods or disrupting their lifecycle, you can validate the self-healing capabilities of your applications, verify readiness/liveness probes, and ensure that your services maintain availability during transient failures.

Understanding Pod Disruptions

Pod disruptions typically simulate common failure modes such as: * Unexpected crashes: Simulating a process failure or OOM (Out of Memory) event. * Scheduling delays: Understanding how long it takes for a replacement pod to become "Ready." * Service Availability: Ensuring that traffic is correctly routed away from terminating pods to healthy replicas.

Prerequisites

Before starting, ensure you have: * A Kubernetes cluster (target cluster). * krknctl installed, or the Krkn Operator deployed. * Access to the krkn-hub image repository.

Hands-on Lab: Executing a Pod-Deletion Experiment

In this lab, we will use krknctl to execute a controlled pod-deletion scenario. This will terminate pods matching specific labels to observe how the deployment controller handles the replacement and service traffic.

Step 1: Identify the Target

Identify the deployment you wish to test. For this exercise, we will target a dummy application in the chaos-test namespace.

# List pods to find your target
kubectl get pods -n chaos-test

Step 2: Configure the Scenario

Pod scenarios in Krkn allow you to define the label_selector to ensure you only disrupt the intended workload. Create a configuration file (or define environment variables) for the pod-scenarios.

# example-pod-chaos.yaml
scenario: pod-scenarios
label_selector: "app=frontend"
namespace: "chaos-test"
duration: 60s
kill_count: 2

Step 3: Execute the Experiment

Using krknctl, trigger the chaos experiment. This abstracts the container runtime complexities and initiates the disruption.

krknctl run pod-scenarios \
  --config example-pod-chaos.yaml

Step 4: Observability and Validation

While the experiment is running, monitor the behavior of the targeted deployment in a separate terminal:

# Monitor the deployment rollout
kubectl get pods -n chaos-test -w

Look for the following indicators: 1. Termination: Verify that pods enter the Terminating state. 2. Re-creation: Verify that the ReplicaSet immediately triggers the creation of new pods. 3. Recovery Time: Measure the duration from the start of the Terminating signal until the new pod reaches the Running and Ready state.

Step 5: Post-Experiment Analysis

Once the execution finishes, inspect the logs generated by Krkn to verify the successful impact of the experiment.

# View scenario status reports
krknctl status --job <job-id>

Troubleshooting Common Issues

  • Pods not terminating: Verify that the label_selector matches the pods exactly. If using an incorrect key/value pair, the experiment may report 0 pods affected.

  • Permission Denied: Ensure that the ServiceAccount used by the Krkn Operator or krknctl has sufficient RBAC permissions (specifically delete and list verbs for the pods resource).

  • Metrics Missing: If monitoring via Prometheus/Grafana, ensure your steady-state metrics are being scraped at a high enough frequency to capture the rapid pod replacement cycle.

Summary

By executing pod-disruption experiments, you move beyond theory and into evidence-based reliability. You have now learned how to target specific pods, trigger controlled deletions, and observe the cluster’s orchestration response. In subsequent sections, we will build upon this by chaining these disruptions into complex, multi-stage failure workflows.
